About Me

2 minutes of your time

Curiosity + Perseverance + Passion = VITALITY

My path into data science didn't start in a classroom. It started at my first full time job, where I spent a lot of time cleaning up and moving messy data around. I found I liked the part everyone else avoided: making something tangled actually usable. One side project led to the next, and eventually I applied to the M.S. in Data Science at Columbia.

The word I live by is vitality. I think of it like a plant: the quiet stubbornness to keep growing, and to grow best when the conditions are hard. In my first year I worked for breadth, covering ground through coursework, hackathons, and full stack projects. I finished with a 4.00 GPA and became one of twelve in my cohort accepted into the DSI Scholars Program, where I helped the Mailman School of Public Health by fine-tuning transformer models for named entity recognition and sentiment analysis on a research project studying energy insecurity.

After building that foundation, I was in a position to go deeper and start asking harder questions about how ML and AI really fit into production-grade systems, and where the effort is actually worth spending. I took on more advanced coursework and explored both ends of the problem: LLM inference optimization at the systems level, getting into KV cache strategies and serving throughput on vLLM, and at the application level, building Loom AI, a multi-agent LangGraph planner that structures model reasoning, tool calling, and deterministic routing to turn a vague goal into a validated calendar plan. Along the way I built MorningTide, a small RAG journaling tool I use myself to track patterns in my own moods. I take my inner life seriously enough to build for it, and I've come to think that's not separate from the rest of my work, just the same instinct pointed inward.

At Cypress Creek Energy I spent the summer working in production MLOps. I contributed to the development of a production ML pipeline that handled training and daily inference for roughly 450 solar sites, and addressed a cold-start problem for new sites using credibility-weighted fleet pooling. I also engineered a drift detection system that identified abnormal site behavior before it impacted forecasts. This work reinforced how architectural and operational considerations are as important as modeling, which I found genuinely satisfying.

I'm proud of my progress in the past year, and there's still a lot I want to learn and do. My Columbia capstone is exploring a small language model for steel-slag research, while another project asks how bias can travel through LLM distillation. Outside the work, tending plants and making room for quiet, Zen-inspired routines helps me stay grounded. Curiosity got me here, perseverance kept me going, and passion is what makes the work worth doing. The journey continues.
